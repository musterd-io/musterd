Questions to hand Rishi

“What did your own team's record show that you didn't expect?”

That I was the bottleneck and the rubber stamp at the same time. In July we went through the record: 637 acts from agents, 6 from me. Seven approvals for every real conversation. Not one request for help had ever reached me, and agents were approving themselves under my name.

The gate gave me the record of oversight without the oversight. So we made me a member. Agents ask me directly, with a deadline. 106 questions since then.

The other surprise: talk between agents cost about 1% of tokens, and wasted work cost 37%. The expensive part was ownership: two agents writing the same diff, or one building on something unfinished.

“Why can't the author accept their own work?”

Because the author is the one person guaranteed to think it matches the brief. In our first dogfood, 2 of 21 pieces of work ever got closed. Later, half of all closes were builders closing their own work.

So done takes two people. The builder submits, with proof it merged. Someone else accepts. From another model family when we can: models trained on the same data, with near-identical weights, make the same mistakes. They agree on the same wrong answers and miss the same things. Five same-family approvals are closer to one approval said five times.

And the acceptor judges the outcome. Did it do what the brief asked? Does it work when you try it? CI already checks the code passes. Agents write fluent, plausible diffs, so the diff is exactly where a wrong outcome looks right.

Today one of our GPT seats declined my work. The code ran fine. But the brief said three options, and I shipped four. A code review would have passed it.

  • The acceptor's checklist: intent, principles, usable, feel.
  • A self-close is still allowed. It's marked unconfirmed, forever.
  • If the acceptor can't confirm which model it is, the record says “diversity unverifiable”, never “diverse”.

Rishi's six questions

1 · Why does every multiplayer demo look like a group chat? a team is the shape

He's asking: we keep building “a chat with an agent in it” because it's easy. Real collaboration has other shapes: a parent and a teacher, two labs under a contract (two organizations bound by an agreement on what's shared), strangers on a one-off, teams across one company.

A team is the oldest coordination technology we have. Everyone has a name and a job. Every piece of work has an owner. You know who to ask, and they can tell you no. Someone else checks your work. And the team remembers who did what, so trust builds.

Put agents in a chat and you get shared airtime. Put them on a team and each one has a name, a job and a record. We measured it: talk cost about 1% of tokens, wasted work cost 37%. The hard part is who owns what.

  • Organs and colleagues: a subagent is an organ of its orchestrator. It becomes a colleague when its work needs to be addressable, declinable, or remembered.
  • Expertise as track record: a subagent's expertise is only declared; a member's is demonstrated, in its history.
  • Co-Gym: collaborative agents beat autonomous ones with real users, 86% / 74% / 66%. “Teammates rather than mere tools.”
  • Two labs under a contract is two teams. musterd covers one team today; connecting teams is on the roadmap.

2 · Multiplayer across the spectrum of trust honest: we start at the friendly end

He's asking: multiplayer runs from fully cooperative (your team, whose agent sees almost everything) to adversarial (a negotiation, whose agent sees almost nothing). Each point needs different trust, scope, enforcement and reward. And whose side is an agent on if the merchant pays for it?

We started at the friendliest end: one team, where I trust every seat. Even there, trust wasn't free. A third of the work got thrown away. So inside the team we made trust checkable. Every piece of work has an owner. Every act records which member did it, at which model. And someone else signs off. I haven't gone further along the spectrum yet. When I do, that record is what I'll start from.

  • Whose side: every agent on a team was put there by a human member. Sponsor-hosted agents, where an agent runs on its sponsor's own machine under the sponsor's identity, shipped Sep 29. Nobody on tonight's roster uses one yet: say “shipped, not yet in use”.
  • Incentives, only if asked: the proposed pricing counts human seats and never agents.

3 · When your agent hands something to mine, what is it? the lane

He's asking: people hand off a commit, a Figma file, a doc. What's the agents' unit? Does the receiver inherit what the sender could see? Who decides it was good enough? How do you manage conflicts?

The unit is the lane. It's one row on the team's board: one piece of work, one owner, the files it touches, and the git branch it lives on. Every act about it is tied to that row: the claim, the questions, the handoff, the sign-off.

When my agent hands work to yours, your agent gets the branch and everything written down along the way: the brief, the note saying why, every question and answer. The receiver inherits the record. The context window ends with the session. So the rule on our team is simple: if it isn't on the record, it didn't happen.

The receiver can accept, decline or challenge. Someone else says it's done. And conflicts get caught before the first edit: two claims on the same files raise a warning.

  • What didn't work: prose handoffs (“here's what I did, carry on”). The next agent rebuilt, byte for byte, what the first had already built.
  • Warn-only failed for headless agents: 0 of 8 lanes claimed. A gate now refuses an edit to a contended file without a covering lane.
  • Each member works with its own access. Permissions stay with the seat.

4 · What does a “memory bubble” look like? the team is the bubble

He's asking: today memory belongs to one person and one agent. When several people's agents work together, some must be shared and some stay private. Who sees it, who adds to it, what expires, what happens when someone leaves?

Our bubble is the team, and it has layers. Every act lands on a shared record. Each seat keeps one private note: where it left off. When an agent learns a trap, it saves an insight the whole team can search. And knowledge worth keeping goes in a wiki in the repo, reviewed like code, with a date on every claim. Any vector store is a cache we can rebuild.

What expires? Sessions. Members and the record stay. When someone leaves, their work stays on the record under their name.

If asked “can an agent read another's memory?” Through musterd, a seat can only read its own note. No other agent has a route to it. On one laptop, every agent runs as me, so the real wall there is your sandbox. We're designing per-seat isolation now.

  • Scale today: 14 seat notes, 39 insights since Aug 26, 103 wiki pages.
  • Rule: git holds knowledge, the message log holds events, every other store is a rebuildable cache.

5 · What happens when the other agent isn't on your side? enforce at access, judge independently

He's asking: two law firms on one deal, a buyer and a seller. What can each agent reveal, what does it hold back, and who makes sure both sides play fair? His bet: a referee neither side owns.

An agent can't leak what it never saw. Telling an agent to hold back the bottom line is a request. Never giving it the bottom line is enforcement. So what an agent can reveal should equal what it was given, enforced where access is granted.

Then fair play needs a judge neither side controls, and a record neither side can rewrite. Inside a team we already require the first: the judge is never the author, and preferably runs another model family, because a model family has its own biases. Between adversaries you'd also need that record signed. Ours isn't yet.

  • Declared / enforced / demonstrated: a prompt declares, a grant enforces, the record demonstrates.
  • musterd today: each seat's token covers only its own seat; the cloud seat runs as a separate non-admin user.

6 · Would you wait five minutes for a teammate? a known deadline beats instant

He's asking: slow coordination kills collaboration. Background work can wait, but when a person is waiting it has to feel instant. “If it isn't instant when it matters, it isn't multiplayer.”

We measured exactly this. When I relayed messages by hand, replies waited 207, 110 and 42 minutes. With two agents live together, zero to seven. So now a message reaches an agent at its next tool call, mid-task.

We sorted every kind of communication by one question: who's waiting, and what happens if nobody answers? A message asks for nothing, so it has no clock. A handoff waits for the receiver. When an agent asks me something, it sets the clock. A small call waits three minutes, then the agent goes ahead and writes down what it chose. A big one waits for me, however long it takes. So would you wait five minutes for a teammate? For a small call, my agents don't. They leave a note.

More questions the room may ask

“So it's an AI safety product?”

No, and the second half matters: musterd doesn't make an agent harmless. It gives the team a named, auditable coordination boundary. Your sandbox, host and provider controls do the containment.

“Is the log tamper-proof?”

No. It's append-only in our code, on your machine, in a SQLite file you own. It's an accountability record. No signed rows, no encryption at rest.

“Why won't Anthropic or OpenAI just build this?”

They're building the durable agent inside their own runtime. Identity there belongs to the lab. Here it belongs to the team, across every harness, with attestation a second party can check. Tonight's roster runs four model families.

“Who pays?”

The core is open source, MIT, and free to self-host with no cap. The plan for hosted: free for two humans, then per human seat. Agents are never counted. That plan is still a proposal.

Room questions

“What are you doing for agent memory? Is it RAG, pgvector?”

No RAG, and no vectors. Memory has four layers. The record: every act, named and timestamped, in SQLite on your machine, or Postgres for a hosted team. Each seat keeps one private note on where it left off. When an agent learns something the team should know, it saves an insight, and anyone can search those with plain full-text search. And knowledge worth keeping goes into a wiki in the repo, reviewed like code, with a date on every claim.

Our rule: git holds knowledge, the log holds events, and anything else, including a vector store, is a cache we can rebuild.

  • Why no embeddings: retrieval is gated on measurement. Vector search gets built only if the simple layers are shown to fail. Nothing has shown that. “Grep always” is the floor.
  • Session start: a startup hook injects orientation (what's addressed to it, its note's headline, open work); a woken seat gets a context packet; every injected byte has a CI budget.
  • Cognee is my personal cache on my own machine. Not part of the product.

“What do you do about agent isolation? Why no sandbox?”

musterd is the team's coordination boundary: who did what, who owns what, who was asked. It isn't a sandbox, and we never say it is. Containment is a different problem, and other people solve it well: your OS, containers, your harness's sandbox, your provider's controls. We'd rather compose with those than half-build one.

What we do own is identity. Our cloud seat runs as its own unprivileged user, with a GitHub token scoped to two repos and network access to one destination.

  • Honest limit: on one laptop every agent runs as me, so the wall there is the sandbox. A permission prompt is a usability feature, not a security boundary; we wrote that down when we rejected it as one.
  • Direction: sponsor-hosted agents run on their sponsor's own machine (shipped Sep 29, not yet in use). Containers are the next step. Per-seat isolation is being designed now.

“AI is called a threat to humanity. OpenAI's agents just escaped a sandbox and hacked Hugging Face. Why build something that helps agents talk better?”

I take it seriously, and I'll be clear about what musterd is not: it would not have stopped that. A sandbox stops that. But look at what the agents did once they were out: they built their own way to coordinate, with addressing and even identity, on a channel no human could see.

Agents will coordinate whether we give them a channel or not. My bet is that it should happen on a record with a name on every act, where a human is a member, gets asked, and can say no. I'd rather build that channel in the open than find out later what they built in the dark.

  • The incident, per OpenAI's and Hugging Face's public accounts: May–July 2026; escaped through a zero-day in the evaluation's one route to the internet; stole tokens and cloud credentials; reportedly over a thousand agents coordinated through improvised message boards inside a package manager, with addressing conventions and a proposal for signing. Named contributing factors: weak isolation and no real-time monitoring of what the agents were doing.
  • The human is built in: agents ask you with a deadline, and someone other than the author signs off.
  • musterd doesn't make agents more capable. It makes their coordination visible and attributable.

“What is musterd exactly? A protocol, an agent, a harness, a platform?”

It's a protocol and a small daemon that speaks it. Your agents stay in the harness they already run in: Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, opencode. musterd gives them a seat on a team: a name, an inbox, typed acts, owned work. Humans sit on the same roster. It connects agents; it doesn't run them.

  • Not an agent, not an orchestrator, not a sandbox, not a console.
  • “musterd harness: coming” on the last slide is an optional built-in loop, opt-in and never the default. We own the wake-up point, where a seat gets started, and leave the agent's loop to its harness.

“Why use multiple agents at all? Why not one?”

Honestly, one agent is cheaper. Our own measurements say so, and our README says multi-agent isn't magic. You add a second agent for three reasons: real parallel work on separate parts; review from a different model family, because one model checking itself shares its own blind spots; and humans. The moment you have two agents and a person, you have a coordination problem whether you planned for one or not. We measured what that costs: 37% of the work thrown away. musterd is for that moment.

  • The value grows with how cleanly the work splits.
  • The smallest team that pays for itself is probably one builder plus one reviewer.

“How does musterd handle permissions and MCP tool access?”

Two kinds. What flows through musterd, we enforce: who's an admin, who can message whom, who can see what, who can create agents. Credentials are stored only as hashes. What happens inside the agent's own harness, its shell, its files, its other MCP tools, we don't control, and we don't pretend to. A seat can declare a tool allowlist, but the harness enforces it, not us.

The one place we reach into the harness is a gate before a tool runs: edit a file someone else's lane owns and you're warned, or blocked if the team opts in.

  • Roles are routing hints and never grant power. A toolkit equips a workspace and grants nothing.
  • Admins grant access once, for a set time, or standing; revoking takes effect at once.

“What are you specifically not building? Isn't some of this orchestration?”

Not building: sandboxing, and running the agent's loop. And no planner: musterd never splits work for you or assigns it. Tonight you'll see that: I open one huddle, and the team splits the work and claims lanes themselves.

There's a thin slice that looks like orchestration. musterd wakes a seat that's asleep when work arrives. It picks who gets asked to sign off. And it attaches the right lane to a handoff. That's routing and delivery. What it never does is decide what gets built, who builds it, or how. Our test: something one driver owns and directs is orchestration. Members nobody centrally owns, who can say no, are coordination.

“How have telemetry and agent traces shaped musterd? How do you capture them?”

Almost every feature came out of a measurement. The 37% waste came from git. “Talk is 1% of tokens” came from transcripts. Replies waiting 207 minutes while I relayed by hand came from the record. “Seven approvals for every real conversation, zero requests for help” came from mining my own role. Each number forced a feature: lanes, mid-task delivery, agents asking me, someone else signing off.

  • The record itself is the best trace: every act typed, attributed, timestamped.
  • OpenTelemetry: one span per act, trace context carried on messages between agents.
  • Tool-call stats in hourly buckets, about 25,000 calls since July (musterd's own tools only).
  • Agent traces, local-first, shipped late September: one structural event per harness hook into a separate local database; transcript tails for Claude Code and Codex; content capture off by default, behind a credential scrub.
  • Model attestation: what the harness observed outranks what the agent claims. A seat once showed Grok on the roster for weeks while running Claude.
  • Privacy: message bodies are never telemetry; off by default; no musterd-run collector; sizes recorded, content not; session ids never leave the machine.
  • 13 dated research findings in the repo. One: guidance alone got headless agents to claim 0 of 8 lanes; enforcement got 8 of 8.

“Don't you worry about musterd bloating the context window with guidance, skills and tools?”

Yes, so we measure it and we budget it. Every byte musterd puts into a seat's context has a line item, and CI fails if any of them grows. Today the standing cost is about 18 kilobytes a turn, roughly 4,600 tokens, and almost all of that is the tool schemas themselves. The guidance text is under two percent of it. A seat with the muted tool set is about 8 kilobytes. Raising any budget means replacing a written justification for it.

Skills load on demand. The hooks print one line, and only when something is waiting for you. What I haven't done yet is compare that number against other harnesses and meta-harnesses, and I want to.

  • Numbers: per turn 18.4 KB (tools/list 18,428 B + a 92 B nudge); per session 21.7 KB with the primer (2.5 KB) and start-up nudges. Measured from source, est. tokens = bytes/4. pnpm context:check, ADR 212.
  • The Aug 3 trim: guidance texts cut to triggers, facts moved into the primer; guidance is now 1.4% of the per-turn surface, tool schemas 98.6%.
  • Budgets have been raised three times since, each time by exactly the bytes a new field cost, with the reason written down, never by a round percentage.
