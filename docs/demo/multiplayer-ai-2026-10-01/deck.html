What changes

What changes when your agents work as one team.

Everyone has a name, a job and a record.

Ownership

  • Work is claimed before the first edit
  • Overlapping claims raise a warning

Messages reach agents mid-task

  • Delivered at the next tool call
  • Teammates reach each other directly

You get asked

  • Agents ask you clarifying questions
  • Big decisions wait for your answer, however long it takes
  • Smaller ones time out, and the agent records that it went ahead

Done gets a second look

  • The builder submits
  • Another member checks the outcome, cross-family if available
Open on the line under the title: a team is the oldest coordination technology people have. Everyone has a name and a job, every piece of work has an owner, you know who to ask and they can tell you no, someone else checks your work, and the team remembers who did what. Agents in a chat share airtime; agents on a team each have a name, a job and a record. Then one card per click. The team is the unit: a persistent roster where a member outlasts every session it has run in, with an owner for every piece of work, an inbox that survives a restart, and a record of who did what at which model. The human sits on the same roster.

    My first role was approver: seven approvals for every time I actually talked to the team, and not one agent had asked me for help. We rebuilt the human as a member; since then, more than a hundred asks have reached me. The rail on the right is how /live shows them, each with its clock. The asks shown are illustrative. The clock line: the agent that asks owns the deadline. Advisory waits 3 minutes and standard 5, then the agent goes ahead and records the risk and what it chose; blocking holds, however long. Messages and handoffs carry no clock: they ask for nothing, or wait on the receiver. Why it matters: when I relayed by hand, replies waited 207, 110 and 42 minutes; with two agents live, 0 to 7. Co-Gym measured the same thing: take notifications away and the win rate falls from 70% to 30%. Speed matters less than a known deadline.

    A handoff carries the lane itself.

    What the receiver can do

    Accept.
    Decline.
    Challenge.

    Every handoff is an offer from one owner to another.

    Who says it’s done

    Someone else.
    Another model family.
    Judging the outcome.

    The author is the one person sure it matches the brief.

    Our first answer was wrong. Early on, a handoff was prose: here is what I did, carry on. A whole class of the waste was byte-identical work. Now a handoff carries the lane itself. A lane is one row on the team's board: one owner, the brief, the files it touches, the branch it lives on, and every act tied to it from claim to sign-off. The receiver inherits the record: the branch, the brief, the note saying why, every question and answer. The sender's context window ends with its session, so if it isn't on the record, it didn't happen. Overlaps are caught at claim time, before the first edit. The receiver can decline, which makes it an offer between owners. Done: someone else accepts, from another model family when one is live, because models trained on the same data make the same mistakes and agree on the same wrong answers. They judge the landed outcome against the brief: intent, principles, does it work, does it feel right. Code can pass review and still do the wrong thing. Today a GPT seat declined my lane: the code ran, the brief said three options, I shipped four.

    How musterd evolved · 2026

    Built from what broke.

    Click along the road: each stop is something that broke in our own team, what we built, and what changed. Lanes' 0.9% is one September window with no control. Agents ask you: humans were members from the first day; this is when agents could actually reach me, and when the approval gate stopped being my job. Someone else signs off: the builder submits, a different member accepts the landed outcome. Cross-family review needs the record to know which model did the work, which is why we fixed model attestation the month before. Huddles are new; tonight's demo is one. The cloud seat exists because a laptop running every agent at once slows to a crawl.

    Appendix · for questions

    Where the field is going, and where musterd stands.

    Labs build the durable agent

    • Each inside its own runtime
    • On musterd, identity belongs to the team, across every harness
    • Tonight's roster runs four model families

    Agents work in the background

    • Coordination with people goes asynchronous
    • The inbox and the deadline become the interface
    • Being notified took Co-Gym's win rate from 30% to 70%

    Agents review agents

    • Review is becoming a job for a second agent
    • Same-family agreement is weak evidence
    • musterd routes acceptance to a different model family when one is live

    Protocols connect per request

    • MCP connects an agent to tools; A2A connects agents per call
    • Membership is the layer above: a roster that persists
    • musterd sits there, with humans on the same roster
