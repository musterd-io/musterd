# Fundraising and pitching: one story, one ledger

Open for a pitch deck or script, an investor email, a forwardable blurb, an
investor update, Q&A prep, or an accelerator or credit-program application.

You draft. The person `team.md` names decides the raise, the terms and who hears
from us, and sends (hard rule 6). SAFE mechanics are background only; never advise on legal
or tax questions.

## The locked core

Every investor artifact reuses one core, word for word:

- **Three lengths of the same line**: about 50 characters (the brand guide's short
  form), one sentence, two sentences. The two-sentence line passes Michael
  Seibel's Email Test: send it to a smart friend and ask them to explain it back;
  any clarifying question means a rewrite. Aim for "80% accurate and 100% clear",
  and cut words such as "platform"
  ([Seibel, YC 2016](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-pitch-your-company)).
  Record who the test reader was and what they said.
- **Three or four vertebrae** — the points a listener will remember. Find them by
  answering: what are we building and for whom, why hasn't it been done before,
  why is it hard, why now
  ([Ralston, YC demo day guide](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/guide-to-demo-day-pitches/)).
- **The numbers ledger**: one table of sources (`team.md` says where). Each row
  has the value, its definition, the date read and the command or link. Artifacts
  quote ledger rows verbatim and nothing else (hard rule 4). Re-read the team's own
  counts before each use, with its count script where it has one.

Keep three spoken lengths of one story, all on the same vertebrae and numbers:
two sentences, about two minutes (five to seven slides), and six minutes (the
standing deck).

## Deck checklist

- Slide 1 says what the product does in plain words. Every slide passes the stranger
  test: someone outside the field understands it at a glance
  ([Hale, YC 2015](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)).
- The strongest true evidence comes straight after slide 1; order the rest from
  most to least impressive (Seibel). Pre-traction, that is the product working
  and our own record.
- One idea per slide, large type.
- Market sized bottom up with the arithmetic shown: customers who would want it ×
  what we could charge. Never "1% of a huge market", never a quoted analyst
  report as the whole argument (Seibel).
- A competition slide. In DocSend's 2021–22 data, more than 70% of successful
  pre-seed decks had one, against 35% of unsuccessful ones
  ([DocSend](https://www.docsend.com/blog/the-pre-seed-round-in-2021-22/)).
  It follows the team's decision on how the field is used and the landscape's
  freshness rule (`positioning-and-gtm.md`).
- The ask: amount, the milestone it buys, the time frame. Seibel estimates that
  in about 70% of pitches the founder never asks; ours always does when the room
  is investors. The amount and terms are the decider's (`team.md`).
- Close on the vertebrae.

## Q&A prep

Keep the deck's "questions to expect" current. Each answer is
direct, short and true. "I don't know yet" with what would tell us is a good
answer. For each expected objection (a lab builds this, open source can't make
money, this is a feature), the answer cites something we have: a PR, an ADR, a
ledger row. Practice by having a second seat ask them cold.

## Process artifacts

- **Target list**: name, firm, why them (a portfolio company, a thesis, a post),
  warm path if any, stage, last touch, next step. In Gompers et al.'s survey of
  885 VCs (2015–16), only about 10% of deals came inbound from the company; most
  came through networks and referrals, so a warm path is worth finding
  ([NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/dec16/how-do-venture-capitalists-make-decisions)).
- **Forwardable blurb**: the two-sentence line, one or two ledger numbers, the
  ask, a link. Written so the person introducing us can forward it unchanged.
- **Follow-ups**: one, with something new in it.
- **Investor update** (monthly): highlights, lowlights (always included),
  metrics against plan from the ledger, what we learned, asks someone can act on.
- **Applications and credit programs**: fetch the live form and terms on the day,
  record the date, and answer each question in as few sentences as it takes.
  Two snapshots of Anthropic's startup program page gave conflicting eligibility
  rules on 2026-10-13 ([claude.com/programs/startups](https://claude.com/programs/startups)).

## Open source metrics, honestly

- Stars are never shown alone. He et al. found about 6.0M suspected fake stars
  across 18,617 repositories, concentrated in AI and LLM projects
  ([arXiv 2412.13459](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13459)). Show usage next to
  them: active teams, installs, issues and PRs from outside the team.
- Seed-stage devtool reference points (Redpoint, 2023, 80 companies): median
  2,850 stars at seed, most seed companies with no revenue
  ([Redpoint](https://www.redpoint.com/content-hub/written/so-how-many-stars-is-enough/)).
  Dated; quote with the year.
- Our own team's use is real and is ours. Say "our team", with the count, and
  never let it read as customers.

## Never

- Invent, round up or annualize a metric.
- Call a conversation a design partner, or interest a letter of intent.
- Imply customers, partners or investors we do not have.
- Present top-down market size, or stars as traction.
- Claim "no competitors".
- Set the raise, the cap, the valuation or any term.
- Contact an investor, or send anything to one.
- Quote a benchmark without its year, or a program's terms without the date read.
